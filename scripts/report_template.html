02

The model has one parameter, not two

The grid search estimates s and c separately. It cannot: they are the same parameter wearing two hats.

The match rule is P(1 beats 2) = c^s1 / (c^s1 + c^s2). Dividing through by c^s1 turns it into a logistic model in the skill difference, and skill differences are Normal(0, s√2). So every outcome depends on the two parameters only through their product:

P = sigmoid( ln(c) · (s₁ − s₂) )　⟹　λ = s · |ln c|

Any two (s, c) pairs on the same λ contour produce an identical distribution of results. The 50×50 grid therefore has a ridge of equally good optima rather than a single best point — which explains a loose end in the original work: the fitted c comes out as the same constant for every league and every season. It is not being estimated at all. c is pinned by the grid, and s silently absorbs every bit of the variation.

03

Three threats to the conclusions

One is a bug, one is a time trend, and one is the size of the league. The third is decisive.

A. The NFL result is a row-ordering artifact

The NFL script builds its dominance series from df['Year'].unique() — file order, descending from 2023 — and its balance series from groupby('Year'), which sorts ascending. Slicing both positionally with [:48] pairs 1970’s balance with 2022’s dominance, 1971’s with 2021’s, and so on down the list.

The published positive NFL relationship is an artifact of the misalignment. Correctly paired, the effect is absent.

B. The NCAAF result is a shared time trend

NCAA football’s dominance measure is a cumulative count of prior top-10 finishes, so it rises with time by construction — year alone explains {{NCAAF_YEAR_R2}} of it. SRS spread also declines over time. Correlating the two measures the trends, not a relationship. Detrend both and R² falls from {{NCAAF_RAW_R2}} to {{NCAAF_DET_R2}} (p = {{NCAAF_DET_P}}).

C. The NBA result is league expansion

This is the one that matters, because the NBA panels are the deck’s strongest. The two metrics that come out significant are precisely the two that are not scale-free. The deck’s win% spread sums |w − .5| across teams without dividing by n, so it grows with the league; HHI has a 1/n floor, so it shrinks with it. Across this sample the NBA went from 23 teams to 30.

05

What machine learning adds

Two questions the original analysis could not ask: does any model predict dominance out of sample, and what actually keeps a team on top?

Season level: no out-of-sample signal

Forward-chaining cross validation — train on the past, predict the future — using all six balance metrics as features. Negative R² means worse than predicting a constant.

Out-of-sample R², forward-chaining CV ml_season_cv.csv
{{ROWS_CV}}
LeagueBaselineLassoElasticNetRandom forest

Every model beats the naive baseline, so there is some information — but none reaches positive R². With 42–54 seasons and six collinear predictors this is what should be expected, and it means the in-sample R² values in the deck are not evidence of prediction.

Team level: strongly predictable

Reframing to ~9,000 team-seasons — given this team’s season, will it be elite next season? — with a strict temporal split, the signal is unambiguous.

The mechanism

Dominance persists because good teams stay good, not because history compounds. Margin of victory this season predicts next season’s elite status far better than a team’s whole record of past elite finishes — and the historical elite rate has negative importance, meaning it actively misleads the model. That is a mechanism the balance hypothesis does not require.

Where the eras actually break

The original analysis splits history by hand at 1981 / 1990 / 2000 / 2003, and its conclusions differ by window. PCA (74% of variance in the first component) with k-means, plus a binary-segmentation change-point scan on the NBA dominance series, puts the breaks at 1988, 1994, 2004 and 2012 instead.

07

Data collection and engineering

The fitting grid is 10.7 million season simulations, and ten raw files that disagree about what a season is.

Making the fit tractable

50 × 50 grid points × 100 replicates × 97 seasons, written as a Python loop over individual matches, is days of compute. Two changes bring it to 82 seconds — and both fall straight out of the λ identity in section 02:

  • Evaluate sigmoid(ln(c) · Δskill) once instead of c**skill twice per match.
  • Resolve an entire season with one matrix product against the schedule’s incidence matrix, batched across replicates.
  • Reuse one block of random draws across the whole grid (common random numbers), which is also what makes the λ ridge visible rather than buried in Monte Carlo noise.

Storage

The NBA labels a season "2000-2001"; everyone else uses 2001. That mismatch is exactly the class of error behind the NFL alignment bug, so the pipeline normalises all ten datasets behind one season dimension table with foreign keys and indices chosen for the access pattern the scripts actually use — one league, a range of years.

08

What this does not settle

The hypothesis may well be true. What the available data cannot do is demonstrate it.

  • Missing leagues. Soccer, MLB and the NHL — the deck’s largest sections — have no data in the repository. The Premier League notebook reads Premier_Teams_Ranking_Enhanced.xlsx, which was never committed. The tie-aware simulator for those sports is implemented and tested; it needs only the files.
  • Power. 42–54 seasons per league is too few to detect a modest effect alongside league-size and time confounds.
  • Design. Season-level correlation cannot separate “balance entrenches elites” from “both respond to expansion, revenue sharing, or the draft.”
Where to go next

The team-level framing is the productive one. It has 200× the sample, an unambiguous signal, and a testable mechanism — persistence of quality rather than entrenchment of position. Adding the missing leagues would let the same question be asked where balance genuinely varies: European soccer has no draft, no salary cap and no revenue sharing, which makes it the natural counterfactual to the American leagues.
