The measure

One number that travels across sports

For each edition, draw a champion uniformly at random from the field that actually competed that year. Repeat 20,000 times. The dominance multiple is the real concentration of titles divided by that null's.

dominance multiple = HHI(real champions) ÷ HHI(champions drawn at random from the same fields)

A multiple of 1 means titles are spread exactly as blind chance among entrants would spread them. Above 1 means a few names recur more than the field size alone can account for. Because the null uses each year's actual field, a sport is never penalised for simply having fewer contenders.

Reading it

Why cycling and not cricket?

The ordering tracks how much a single competitor controls the outcome, and how long the contest runs.

  • Tour de France (3.14×) — three weeks of racing, where a single rider's aerobic ceiling and a team built entirely around them compounds over eighty hours. The longest event in the set is the most dominated.
  • NCAA football (2.89×) — no salary cap, no draft, and recruiting that feeds on itself. Success is the recruiting pitch, so the advantage is self-reinforcing in a way professional leagues deliberately prevent.
  • NBA (2.23×) — a draft and a cap push toward parity, but five players on the floor means one exceptional player moves the outcome more than in any other major team sport.
  • Cricket and the Women's World Cup (1.70×, 2.09×) — short knockout tournaments held every four years. Few editions, high variance, and the least room for an advantage to compound.
  • Masters and tennis majors (1.71×, 1.79×) — large fields and four-day formats, where one bad round ends it. Golf's variance is the great leveller even when the talent gap is not.
The pattern

Event length and individual leverage predict dominance better than competitive-balance policy does. The two least regulated competitions here — a bike race and college football — are the two most concentrated, and the sports with the most deliberate parity machinery sit in the middle, not the bottom.

Limits

Where the method breaks

The null needs to know who could have won. Where the eligible field is unobservable, the multiple is meaningless — and the Kentucky Derby shows exactly that.

A horse runs the Derby once, so the only durable unit is the sire. Across {{DERBY_N}} runnings there are {{DERBY_UNIQUE}} distinct sires, and the most successful accounts for {{DERBY_TOP}}% of winners — effectively no durable dominance at all. But a sire is only eligible in the years he has three-year-olds running, and that field is not in the data. A uniform-over-all-sires null would let every sire win every year, which produces a multiple below 1 and should not be read as a finding. It is reported here descriptively and excluded from the table above.

Method

Concentration is the Herfindahl index of title shares. The null draws one champion per edition uniformly from that edition's observed field, 20,000 times, preserving the real sequence of field sizes.
