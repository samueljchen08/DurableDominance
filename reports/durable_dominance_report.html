Dominance

Fourteen franchises won the last forty-three NBA championships. Run the same seasons through a random walk driven by each team's own measured strength and you get twenty. Championships are roughly twice as concentrated as the teams' quality can explain — and the gap is a multi-season effect, not playoff luck.

The clustering is the phenomenon. Champions arrive already decorated — the average winner holds 2.1 prior titles and 5.1 prior top-four finishes, with a 0.571 career record. Only 14 of 43 were first-time winners.

Study A

Do tight leagues crown veterans?

Six measures of how decorated each champion was, against eight measures of how balanced its league was that year. Eighty-four correlations, so they are judged against a false-discovery correction rather than raw p-values.

Both sides drift over time — pedigree counts accumulate mechanically, and expansion changes the spread of records — so each pair is detrended on year before testing. Of 84 pairs, 8 are significant raw and 7 after detrending, against roughly four expected by chance. Three survive Benjamini-Hochberg.

Imbalance and entrenchment travel together — the opposite of the idea that competitive balance reinforces an elite. Nothing survives in the NBA, whose champions are decorated regardless of how tight the league was.

Study B

Championships against a random walk

A postseason is a short random walk. Each series is seven weighted coin flips; win four and advance. Feed in each season's real strengths and you get the title distribution a league should produce if nothing carried across seasons except visible quality.

Strength comes from a Bradley-Terry fit to the complete head-to-head matrix, so a team is credited for who it beat rather than only how often it won. The matrix reconciles exactly with the standings — 82 games per team, zero mismatches.

P(i beats j) = σ( βi − βj + home court )　·　series = best of 7

First, is the model any good?

Scaling every strength gap by a temperature γ and maximising the likelihood of the 43 champions that actually happened gives γ = 1.1. The raw fitted gaps are already right. The reliability curve confirms it across the whole range, favourites included.

Study C

Where does the excess come from?

A puzzle: the simulation already uses real strengths, and its per-season predictions are calibrated. So how can the 43-season record be so much more concentrated?

The resolution

Calibration is a marginal property — each season's probability is right on average. Concentration is a joint one. Both hold at once if championship outcomes are positively correlated across seasons for the same franchise, beyond what measured strength explains. That correlation is exactly what durable dominance means, and it is what the single-season model cannot contain.

Quality is persistent — and already priced in

Lag-1 autocorrelation of team strength is ρ = 0.58, a half-life of about 1.3 seasons. But within-franchise variation (SD 0.61) dwarfs between-franchise variation (SD 0.23): franchises cycle rather than being permanently good. And because the simulation uses each season's real strengths, all of this is already inside it.

Is beating your strength a stable trait?

Splitting the record in half and correlating each franchise's excess titles across the two halves gives r = +0.24, p = 0.23 across 28 franchises. Positive, but not significant — suggestive of a persistent conversion skill, and well short of establishing one.

The tension worth noticing

Study B says the aggregate record is more concentrated than strength explains. Study D says you cannot predict which franchises will beat their strength — a history-only model reaches AUC 0.54 out of sample, and the correlation between prior titles and the simulation residual is +0.06 (p = 0.37). Both results are real. Durable dominance shows up reliably in the aggregate and dissolves when you ask it to name names.

Conclusions

What the record supports

  1. Durable dominance is real and measurable. NBA championships are 1.64× more concentrated than the teams' own measured quality can explain, and every sport tested is 1.7–3.1× more concentrated than chance among its own field.
  2. It is a multi-season effect, not playoff luck. The single-season model is well calibrated; what it misses is the correlation of outcomes across seasons for the same franchise.
  3. The mechanism is not identified. Over-performance does not replicate across halves of history (p = 0.23) and is not predictable out of sample. Continuity of personnel, elite teams coasting in the regular season, playoff-specific quality, and a short 43-season record in which a few unusual runs dominate the statistics all remain live.
  4. Imbalance and entrenchment travel together where detectable at all, which is the opposite of the idea that competitive balance reinforces an elite.
  5. The modern NBA no longer shows it. 2010–2023 matches its simulation exactly.
What would settle it

Playoff series data. Everything here infers postseason behaviour from regular-season strength; actual series results would let the model be tested where it matters rather than calibrated to one outcome per year. Roster continuity. The leading candidate mechanism — dynasties keeping personnel the standings cannot see — is directly measurable and absent from this data. More leagues with verified champions. The concentration test needs long records to have power, which is why the era splits individually do not reach significance.

Method

Bradley-Terry strengths by penalised MLE on the full head-to-head matrix; 16-team bracket held constant across eras; best-of-7 as the majority of seven weighted Bernoulli draws, which is exact.

Verification

22 tests in tests/, including the simulator checked against the closed-form best-of-7 probability at five parameter settings, and a check that no pedigree measure can see its own season.
